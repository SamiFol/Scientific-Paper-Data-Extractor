## Discussion and Limitations

The introduction of Cognitive Orchestration presents a shift toward recursive memory synthesis, yet this architecture introduces distinct challenges regarding scalability, ethical deployment, and computational overhead. While the framework addresses the limitations in tool-chaining identified in [Schick et al., 2023] and the reasoning-acting gap noted in [Yao et al., 2023], the reliance on a recursive tuple-based memory update mechanism, $M = (t, c, a, o, r)$, necessitates a careful examination of long-term system performance.

Scalability remains a primary concern for the proposed framework. Given that the attention complexity of the underlying Transformer architecture is $O(N^2)$ per step [Vaswani et al., 2017], the recursive nature of Cognitive Orchestration—where the agent must retrieve and synthesize historical tuples before each action—implies that inference cost scales linearly with chain depth. As the memory bank grows, the retrieval score $S(q, m) = w_1 \cdot \text{sim}(q, c) + w_2 \cdot \exp(-\lambda \cdot (t_{\text{now}} - t_m))$ must be computed efficiently. If the number of memory objects $M$ becomes large, the retrieval process may become a bottleneck, potentially necessitating approximate nearest neighbor search or hierarchical memory structures to maintain real-time performance. Furthermore, while we hypothesize that the reflection component $r$ improves coherence, the accumulation of reflections over long durations could introduce noise into the context window, potentially degrading the model's ability to maintain focus on the primary goal.

Ethical considerations are paramount when deploying autonomous agents capable of persistent memory and recursive reflection. The ability of an agent to synthesize past observations into higher-level reflections, as formalized in our framework, mirrors the social dynamics observed in [Park et al., 2023]. This capability carries significant risks, including the potential for agents to develop biased reflections or perpetuate stereotypes based on the data retrieved from the memory stream. Because the framework relies on prompting-based training to maximize the coherence of $r$ given $o$, there is a risk that the agent may prioritize internal logical consistency over factual accuracy or safety constraints. We emphasize that rigorous human-in-the-loop design is necessary to ensure that the reflection process remains aligned with safety guidelines, particularly as these agents are integrated into interactive environments where they might influence human users.

Future directions for Cognitive Orchestration involve moving beyond the current prompting-based training objective. While we focus on maximizing the coherence of $r$, future work could investigate self-supervised fine-tuning to optimize the weights $w_1$ and $w_2$ within the retrieval score $S(q, m)$, allowing the model to dynamically adjust its retrieval strategy based on task requirements. Additionally, investigating the integration of formal verification methods for the tool-chaining process could mitigate the risk of error propagation across multi-step reasoning chains. By addressing these limitations and refining the memory-reflection loop, we believe Cognitive Orchestration can provide a robust foundation for building autonomous systems that are both capable of complex, multi-step reasoning and grounded in long-term behavioral coherence.
