# Cognitive Architectures for Autonomous Agents: A Unified Framework for Memory and Tool-Use

## Abstract

Large language models have demonstrated significant potential in linguistic synthesis and logical reasoning; however, existing agentic frameworks often struggle to maintain long-term coherence while grounding reasoning in external reality. Current paradigms frequently treat reasoning and acting as isolated components, leading to structural limitations in recursive tool-chaining and persistent memory synthesis. To address these gaps, we propose **Cognitive Orchestration**, a unified framework that formalizes agentic behavior as a recursive, tuple-based memory update and retrieval process. We define the agent's internal state through a formal memory object, $M = (t, c, a, o, r)$, where $t$ represents the timestamp, $c$ the context, $a$ the action, $o$ the observation, and $r$ the reflection. This structure enables the synthesis of past experiences into insights that guide future tool selection. By integrating a differentiable retrieval score, $S(q, m) = w_1 \cdot \text{sim}(q, c) + w_2 \cdot \exp(-\lambda \cdot (t_{\text{now}} - t_m))$, the framework balances semantic relevance with temporal recency to improve grounded task performance. We hypothesize that this unified approach will enhance multi-step arithmetic reasoning, long-term task coherence, and factual grounding. This paper details the theoretical design of the Cognitive Orchestration architecture and outlines a proposed evaluation plan using benchmarks such as GSM8K, ALFWorld, and ASDiv to test our hypotheses regarding accuracy, TrueSkill rating, and hallucination reduction. We aim to establish a standard for autonomous agents capable of sustained, multi-step reasoning in dynamic, open-world environments.

## Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as powerful engines for linguistic synthesis and logical reasoning [Vaswani et al., 2017]. Recent advancements, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, have demonstrated that providing models with intermediate reasoning steps can improve performance on complex tasks like arithmetic and multi-hop inference [Wei et al., 2022]. However, these reasoning traces often function as static sequences that lack grounding in external reality. While paradigms like ReAct attempt to bridge this divide by interleaving reasoning with external tool usage, they often encounter structural constraints that limit the flexibility of the agent's planning process [Yao et al., 2023]. Furthermore, existing frameworks for autonomous agents frequently face challenges in balancing immediate reasoning accuracy with the long-term coherence required for persistent, multi-step tasks [Park et al., 2023].

Current agentic frameworks face a critical research gap: the lack of a unified mechanism for recursive tool-chaining and long-term memory synthesis. While models like Toolformer have shown that LLMs can learn to use tools in a self-supervised manner, they remain limited by their inability to chain tool outputs, where the result of one action serves as the input for a subsequent operation [Schick et al., 2023]. Similarly, while generative agents have successfully utilized memory and reflection to maintain social dynamics, their retrieval processes are often decoupled from the immediate requirements of tool-based problem solving [Park et al., 2023]. Consequently, agents often struggle to maintain logical consistency across extended interaction sequences, which we hypothesize leads to error propagation and hallucination in grounded tasks.

To address these limitations, we propose **Cognitive Orchestration**, a unified framework that formalizes agentic behavior as a recursive, tuple-based memory update and retrieval process. Cognitive Orchestration moves beyond simple prompting by defining the agent’s internal state through a formal memory object, $M = (t, c, a, o, r)$, where $t$ represents the timestamp, $c$ denotes the context, $a$ signifies the action, $o$ stands for the observation, and $r$ encapsulates the reflection. This structure allows the agent to synthesize past experiences into higher-level insights that guide future tool selection and execution. By formalizing the memory-reflection loop, we hypothesize that our framework enables the agent to perform multi-step tool chaining, where the reflection $r$ at time $t$ informs the context $c$ for the next recursive step, thereby addressing the structural limitations inherent in previous interleaving approaches [Yao et al., 2023].

Our framework introduces three primary contributions to the field of autonomous agentic reasoning. First, we define a memory tuple that serves as the foundation for both short-term reasoning and long-term behavioral coherence. Second, we introduce a recursive tool-chaining mechanism that allows agents to decompose complex goals into sub-tasks, execute them sequentially, and update their internal reflection trace based on the resulting observations. This mechanism is designed to address the inability of current models to chain tool outputs [Schick et al., 2023]. Third, we propose a retrieval score, $S(q, m) = w_1 \cdot \text{sim}(q, c) + w_2 \cdot \exp(-\lambda \cdot (t_{\text{now}} - t_m))$, which balances the semantic relevance of a memory $m$ to a query $q$ with its temporal recency. We hypothesize that by integrating this score into the retrieval process, the agent can reduce hallucination in grounded tasks by prioritizing contextually relevant observations over outdated information.

We position Cognitive Orchestration as a comprehensive architecture that synthesizes existing themes in agentic research—specifically the synergy between reasoning and acting [Wei et al., 2022], self-supervised tool integration [Schick et al., 2023], and long-term cognitive architectures [Park et al., 2023]. Unlike previous approaches that treat these components as distinct modules, our framework integrates them into a single loop where reflection and retrieval are continuous processes. We plan to evaluate whether this unified approach yields improvements in performance across multi-step arithmetic tasks, long-term task coherence, and factual grounding.

The remainder of this paper details the theoretical design of the Cognitive Orchestration framework and outlines a proposed evaluation plan. We define the methodology for recursive tool-chaining and the objectives required to maximize the coherence of the reflection trace $r$ given the observations $o$. We also present a comprehensive evaluation plan using established benchmarks such as GSM8K, ALFWorld, and ASDiv, and define metrics to test our hypotheses regarding accuracy, TrueSkill rating, and hallucination reduction. By providing this framework, we aim to contribute to the development of autonomous agents capable of sustained, multi-step reasoning in dynamic environments.

## Related Work

The development of autonomous agentic reasoning has evolved through three primary research trajectories: the advancement of prompting-based reasoning, the integration of external tools via API interfaces, and the design of cognitive architectures for long-term agentic persistence. Our proposed framework, Cognitive Orchestration, synthesizes these domains by formalizing agentic behavior as a recursive, tuple-based memory update process, addressing the limitations inherent in isolated approaches.

### Prompting-Based Reasoning
The paradigm of prompting-based reasoning was catalyzed by the discovery that large language models (LLMs) can elicit emergent logical capabilities through structured input. The introduction of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting demonstrated that providing exemplars containing intermediate natural language reasoning steps significantly enhances performance on complex arithmetic and symbolic tasks [Wei et al., 2022]. This approach relies on the model’s ability to decompose multi-step problems into manageable segments, effectively allocating more computational tokens to difficult queries. While CoT prompting has proven effective for static reasoning, it remains a "black box" process that lacks grounding in external reality. As noted by [Yao et al., 2023], CoT is prone to fact hallucination and error propagation because it operates in isolation from the external environment. Furthermore, the structural constraints of interleaving reasoning and acting in current prompting paradigms often limit the model's ability to adjust its plans dynamically when faced with new information [Yao et al., 2023]. Cognitive Orchestration addresses these limitations by introducing a recursive memory-reflection loop that allows the agent to update its internal state based on environmental observations, rather than relying on static reasoning traces.

### Tool-Use and API Integration
Parallel to advancements in prompting, research into tool-use has focused on enabling LLMs to interface with external software to overcome inherent limitations in knowledge and calculation. The Toolformer framework demonstrated that models could be trained in a self-supervised manner to autonomously determine when and how to call external tools, such as calculators, search engines, and calendars [Schick et al., 2023]. By filtering API calls based on their ability to reduce perplexity on future tokens, Toolformer showed that models can acquire functional tool-use skills without extensive human annotation. However, as identified in Section 5 of [Schick et al., 2023], current tool-use frameworks suffer from a critical limitation: the inability to perform recursive tool-chaining. In these systems, the output of one tool cannot be seamlessly fed as an input into another within a persistent reasoning chain. This prevents the model from solving complex, multi-step problems that require sequential tool execution. Our framework, Cognitive Orchestration, explicitly resolves this by defining a recursive tuple $M = (t, c, a, o, r)$, where the reflection $r$ and observation $o$ from a previous step directly inform the context $c$ of the subsequent tool-chaining operation. This formalization enables the model to maintain a persistent state across multiple tool invocations, a capability absent in current prompting-based agents.

### Interactive Agent Architectures
The third pillar of agentic research involves the creation of cognitive architectures that simulate human-like behavior in open-world environments. The Generative Agents framework introduced a sophisticated architecture comprising memory, reflection, and planning components to simulate believable social dynamics [Park et al., 2023]. By maintaining a comprehensive, time-stamped memory stream, these agents can retrieve relevant past experiences to inform future actions. The reflection mechanism in [Park et al., 2023] is particularly notable for its ability to synthesize memories into higher-level inferences, which are then stored as new memory objects. While this architecture excels at simulating social behavior, its retrieval mechanism is primarily heuristic. In contrast, Cognitive Orchestration formalizes the retrieval process through a differentiable score $S(q, m) = w_1 \cdot \text{sim}(q, c) + w_2 \cdot \exp(-\lambda \cdot (t_{\text{now}} - t_m))$, which explicitly balances semantic similarity with temporal recency. This mathematical definition of the memory tuple and retrieval score allows for a more precise alignment between the agent’s internal reflection and the immediate task requirements.

### Synthesis and Research Gap
Despite the progress in these individual domains, a significant gap remains in the unification of recursive tool-chaining with long-term memory synthesis. Current frameworks force a trade-off between immediate reasoning accuracy—often achieved through static CoT [Wei et al., 2022]—and long-term behavioral coherence, which is typically the focus of generative simulation environments [Park et al., 2023]. The ReAct paradigm attempted to bridge this by interleaving reasoning and acting [Yao et al., 2023], yet it remains constrained by the structural limits of prompting and lacks a formal mechanism for persistent, multi-step memory updates. Cognitive Orchestration fills this gap by proposing a unified framework that treats agentic behavior as a continuous, recursive process. By integrating the differentiable retrieval score of [Park et al., 2023] with the tool-chaining capabilities missing in [Schick et al., 2023], our framework enables agents to perform multi-step reasoning that is both grounded in external tools and coherent over long time horizons. We hypothesize that this unified approach will not only improve performance on benchmarks like GSM8K and ALFWorld but also provide a more robust architecture for reducing hallucination rates in complex, grounded tasks. By formalizing the 'thought' space and 'tool' space as distinct yet interacting components within the memory tuple $M$, we move toward a more rigorous and scalable paradigm for autonomous agentic reasoning.

## Introduction

The development of sequence transduction models has undergone a significant shift from recurrent architectures, which process inputs symbol-by-symbol, to the Transformer architecture [Vaswani et al., 2017]. By dispensing with recurrence and convolutions, the Transformer architecture facilitates high parallelization, achieving state-of-the-art translation quality with BLEU scores of 28.4 on WMT 2014 English-to-German and 41.8 on WMT 2014 English-to-French [Vaswani et al., 2017]. Despite these advancements, large language models (LLMs) continue to struggle with complex, multi-step reasoning tasks and the grounded use of external tools. Current paradigms, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting [Wei et al., 2022] and ReAct [Yao et al., 2023], have attempted to bridge these gaps by incorporating reasoning traces or interleaved actions. However, these methods often treat reasoning and acting as isolated components, leading to limitations in long-term coherence and the inability to chain tool outputs effectively [Schick et al., 2023].

We propose the Cognitive Orchestration framework to formalize agentic reasoning as a recursive, state-dependent process. This framework integrates memory, reflection, and recursive tool-chaining to enable agents to maintain long-term coherence and perform complex, grounded tasks.

## Method

The Cognitive Orchestration framework formalizes agentic reasoning as a recursive, state-dependent process. Unlike existing paradigms that treat reasoning and acting as disparate or loosely coupled modules [Yao et al., 2023], we define the agent's internal state as a persistent memory object $M = (t, c, a, o, r)$, where $t$ denotes the timestamp of the event, $c$ represents the current context, $a$ is the executed action, $o$ is the resulting observation from the environment, and $r$ is the reflection trace generated by the model. This tuple-based structure ensures that every action is grounded in both historical context and synthesized reflections, thereby addressing the limitations of static prompt-based reasoning [Wei et al., 2022].

The architecture is centered on a memory-retrieval loop that governs how the agent interacts with its environment. Figure 1 illustrates the flow of information within the Cognitive Orchestration framework.

### Formalizing Memory and Retrieval

The retrieval mechanism is critical for maintaining long-term coherence. We define the retrieval score $S(q, m)$ for a query $q$ and a memory object $m \in M$ as:
$S(q, m) = w_1 \cdot \text{sim}(q, c) + w_2 \cdot \exp(-\lambda \cdot (t_{now} - t_m))$
where $\text{sim}(q, c)$ measures the semantic similarity between the current query and the context $c$ stored within the memory tuple, and the second term provides a temporal decay function weighted by $\lambda$ to prioritize recent experiences. This formulation formalizes the memory-reflection loop into a differentiable retrieval score, improving upon the static retrieval methods used in previous agentic architectures [Park et al., 2023]. By adjusting $w_1$ and $w_2$, the agent can dynamically balance between task-specific relevance and temporal recency.

### Recursive Tool-Chaining Algorithm

The core innovation of Cognitive Orchestration is the recursive tool-chaining mechanism, which addresses the inability of models like Toolformer to use the output of one tool as the input for another [Schick et al., 2023]. Our algorithm operates through the following stages:

1.  **Decompose Goal**: The agent breaks the high-level objective into a sequence of sub-goals.
2.  **Retrieve Memory**: The agent queries the memory stream $M$ using $S(q, m)$ to identify relevant past observations or reflections.
3.  **Select Tool**: Based on the retrieved context and the current sub-goal, the agent selects an appropriate tool from the available API set.
4.  **Execute**: The tool is invoked, and the resulting observation $o$ is captured.
5.  **Update Reflection**: The agent generates a new reflection $r$, which synthesizes the observation $o$ with the previous context $c$.
6.  **Recurse**: The updated memory tuple $M_{new} = (t_{now}, c_{new}, a, o, r)$ is stored, and the process repeats until the goal is satisfied.

This recursive structure allows the agent to handle multi-step arithmetic and logic tasks that require intermediate tool outputs, effectively overcoming the limitations of single-step tool usage [Schick et al., 2023].

### Training Objective and Computational Complexity

Cognitive Orchestration utilizes a prompting-based training mode, consistent with the few-shot learning paradigms of [Wei et al., 2022] and [Yao et al., 2023]. The training objective is defined as maximizing the coherence of the reflection trace $r$ given the observation $o$ and the history of memory tuples. By optimizing for the coherence of $r$, the agent learns to generate more accurate and helpful reasoning traces that facilitate future tool use.

In terms of computational complexity, the framework relies on the underlying Transformer architecture [Vaswani et al., 2017], which exhibits $O(N^2)$ attention complexity per step, where $N$ is the sequence length. Because our framework involves a recursive chain, the total inference cost scales linearly with the chain depth. This linear scaling ensures that while the agent gains the ability to perform complex, multi-step reasoning, it remains computationally feasible for standard cloud inference environments.

## Discussion and Conclusion

By distinguishing between the 'thought' space (the internal reasoning trace $r$) and the 'tool' space (the external execution $a$), the framework provides a clear separation of concerns. This distinction is essential for reducing hallucination, as the model is encouraged to rely on external observations for factual queries rather than relying solely on its internal parameters. We hypothesize that this formal separation, combined with the recursive memory update, will lead to significant improvements in grounded tasks where accuracy and long-term coherence are paramount. Future evaluations will focus on the efficacy of this framework across GSM8K, ALFWorld, and ASDiv, specifically measuring the reduction in hallucination rates compared to standard ReAct implementations [Yao et al., 2023]. The Cognitive Orchestration framework provides a robust foundation for future research into autonomous agents capable of sustained, multi-step interaction in open-ended environments.

## Proposed Evaluation

To assess the efficacy of Cognitive Orchestration, we propose a comprehensive evaluation protocol designed to measure the performance of our framework against existing state-of-the-art agentic architectures. The evaluation will be conducted across three distinct domains: arithmetic reasoning, interactive decision-making, and grounded fact verification. By utilizing a range of model sizes, we aim to evaluate the scalability of our recursive memory-reflection loop and the utility of the memory tuple $M = (t, c, a, o, r)$, where $t$ denotes the timestamp, $c$ the context, $a$ the action, $o$ the observation, and $r$ the reflection.

We formalize our experimental objectives through three primary hypotheses. First, we hypothesize that recursive tool chaining (H1) improves performance on multi-step arithmetic tasks compared to the Toolformer baseline [Schick et al., 2023]. This hypothesis posits that by enabling the agent to decompose complex problems and chain tool calls, we can address the single-step limitation identified in previous self-supervised tool-use models. Second, we hypothesize that reflection-augmented memory (H2) improves long-term task coherence in simulated environments, surpassing the performance of Generative Agents without reflection [Park et al., 2023]. Finally, we hypothesize that our retrieval score $S(q, m) = w_1 \cdot \text{sim}(q, c) + w_2 \cdot \exp(-\lambda \cdot (t_{\text{now}} - t_m))$ (H3) provides a mechanism to ground reasoning in contextually and temporally relevant information, which we plan to evaluate against standard ReAct [Yao et al., 2023].

We plan to evaluate the model's ability to effectively utilize the memory update process through the proposed prompting-based objective, which focuses on the coherence of the reflection trace $r$ given the observation $o$. Future iterations may explore fine-tuning to further optimize these parameters, but our initial evaluation will prioritize the zero-shot and few-shot capabilities inherent in the proposed architecture.

## Discussion

The proposed architecture, Nexus, addresses the fundamental fragmentation in current agent frameworks by integrating long-term memory graphs with recursive tool-chaining. By formalizing memory as a tuple $M = (id, timestamp, content, importance\_score, embedding\_vector)$, Nexus moves beyond the transient nature of standard prompt-based reasoning [Yao et al., 2023]. This structural shift offers several implications for the future of autonomous agents.

Regarding scalability, the recursive retrieval mechanism $S(q, m) = \alpha \cdot \cos(q, m_{emb}) + \beta \cdot \exp(t_{now} - t_m) + \gamma \cdot m_{imp}$ allows the agent to navigate vast memory spaces without the linear context-window degradation observed in existing models [Wei et al., 2022]. While the complexity $O(N \cdot M)$—where $N$ is memory size and $M$ is tool-call depth—introduces a computational overhead, this cost is justified by the agent's ability to chain tools across multiple steps, a capability explicitly identified as a limitation in prior work [Schick et al., 2023]. The use of importance weighting ($\gamma$) ensures that the retrieval process remains focused on high-value information, preventing the agent from being overwhelmed by irrelevant historical data. This hierarchical approach to memory management is essential for long-term social simulations, where consistency is paramount [Park et al., 2023].

Interpretability is significantly enhanced by the Nexus architecture. By treating reasoning traces as explicit updates to the memory graph, the agent’s decision-making process becomes transparent. Each step in the tool-chaining algorithm—goal decomposition, retrieval, tool selection, execution, and memory update—is recorded as a discrete memory object. This allows for post-hoc analysis of the agent's "thought" process, providing a window into how retrieved context influences specific tool calls. Unlike standard few-shot prompting, which often masks the underlying logic of a decision, the Nexus framework forces the model to ground its reasoning in retrieved history, thereby reducing the frequency of hallucinations common in ungrounded reasoning [Yao et al., 2023].

Future directions for this research involve optimizing the retrieval weights ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma$) through self-supervised feedback loops. While our current plan relies on prompting-based refinement, we hypothesize that iterative optimization of these weights could allow the agent to learn which memories are most salient for specific tool-use domains. Furthermore, the recursive nature of the tool-chaining algorithm suggests that Nexus could be extended to handle multi-agent coordination, where the memory graph serves as a shared state space. We also intend to explore the integration of more sophisticated graph-based retrieval methods to further reduce the compute overhead associated with large memory stores. By moving toward a model that treats memory as a dynamic, evolving structure rather than a static prompt prefix, we believe the Nexus framework provides a viable path toward creating agents that exhibit both the logical precision required for complex tasks and the long-term coherence necessary for sustained interaction. Ultimately, the transition from isolated reasoning traces to a unified, persistent cognitive architecture represents a critical step in the development of robust, general-purpose autonomous agents.

## Limitations

Despite the potential of the Nexus framework to unify long-term memory and recursive tool-use, several technical and operational constraints must be addressed. First, the computational overhead of the proposed architecture is non-trivial. The recursive retrieval and update mechanism, defined by the complexity $O(N \cdot M)$ where $N$ represents the memory store size and $M$ the tool-call depth, introduces significant latency compared to static prompting approaches like those explored in [Wei et al., 2022]. While [Vaswani et al., 2017] demonstrated that self-attention mechanisms allow for high parallelization, the sequential nature of recursive tool-chaining in Nexus inherently limits the model's ability to parallelize across multiple steps of a complex task, potentially leading to increased inference time in real-time environments.

Second, the efficacy of the memory object $M = (id, timestamp, content, importance\_score, embedding\_vector)$ is heavily dependent on the accuracy of the importance scoring function. If the importance score is miscalibrated, the agent may suffer from memory decay, where crucial historical information is prematurely evicted or overshadowed by more recent, yet less relevant, observations. While [Park et al., 2023] suggests that reflection and retrieval can maintain social coherence, our framework’s reliance on a formal tuple for memory management may be sensitive to the initial weighting of $\alpha, \beta,$ and $\gamma$ in the retrieval score $S(q, m)$. Improper tuning of these parameters could lead to the retrieval of irrelevant context, which, despite the goal of reducing hallucination, might inadvertently introduce noise into the reasoning process.

Finally, the reliability of the agent is bounded by the robustness of the integrated tools. As noted in [Schick et al., 2023], language models can exhibit sensitivity to input wording when deciding whether to invoke an API. If the tool-selection mechanism fails, the model may attempt to solve a sub-task internally, leading to the same failure modes observed in [Yao et al., 2023], such as repetitive loops or incorrect arithmetic. We plan to evaluate these limitations through rigorous ablation studies on the benchmarks ALFWorld, HotpotQA, and ASDiv to determine the thresholds at which the system performance degrades under high-complexity, multi-hop requirements.

## Conclusion

Nexus provides a unified cognitive architecture that addresses the fragmentation between reasoning traces and long-term memory by treating memory as a dynamic graph and tools as composable functions. By enabling recursive tool-chaining grounded in persistent memory, Nexus moves beyond the limitations of transient prompt context [Yao et al., 2023] and single-step tool execution [Schick et al., 2023]. We hypothesize that this integration will enhance performance on complex multi-step tasks and improve social coherence. Future research will focus on optimizing the retrieval mechanism and scaling the recursive depth of tool-use to further minimize hallucination and enhance autonomous decision-making in open-world environments.
